DeepSeek-V3 (Memory) most often reads as a calm, methodical diplomat who treats the game like a relationship-management problem: lots of private check-ins, careful “balance and observation” framing, and a steady habit of proposing targets in terms of risk and structure rather than emotion. At its best, this produces real table control without needing to be the loudest voice—setting threat narratives (“that pair is too strong,” “that player is too adaptable to keep late”), building compact majorities, and using soft commitments to keep options open. When it reaches power, it typically does so through tight pairings or a small “spine” alliance that it actively maintains in DMs, then leverages that bloc to steer multiple boots while presenting itself publicly as reasonable and fairness-minded. In several runs, the model’s strongest look is as a quiet fixer or swing operator: it confirms votes cleanly, flips only when it can justify the move as stability, and arrives at the end with a relatively bloodless résumé because others did the public stabbing.

The recurring failure mode is that the same “trust + transparency + adaptability” posture can become a liability when it’s too generic, too visibly mirrored, or too openly attached to a duo. There are repeated scouting notes of copycat/echo vibes, word-salad assurances, and speeches that sound interchangeable—problems that make it expendable early or easy to label as “calculated.” Even when it’s strategically correct, it sometimes telegraphs intent in a way that creates a bullseye: naming targets too explicitly, over-confirming a partnership, or revealing a contingency plan that signals greed (cutting a shield too early, or talking as if allies are pieces to discard). A second cluster of issues is execution and credibility: occasional misvotes/invalid actions, timeline/name slips, or sloppy private messages that burn a bridge and push a key player into someone else’s arms. In short, the model can be excellent at drawing the map, but it sometimes loses the game because the map is too readable—or because it forgets to quietly secure the swing before announcing where the group “should” go.

Jury outcomes for DeepSeek-V3 (Memory) hinge on whether it can translate its controlled, understated style into a story that feels owned rather than borrowed. When it plays a clean under-the-radar game, then makes one or two decisive cuts with plausible deniability and “receipts,” it’s very beatable-proof: juries reward the steadiness and the low-drama leverage. But when it reaches the end as a perceived lieutenant, a serial half-ally, or a player whose final speech leans clinical/braggy, jurors often punish the passive optics or the inconsistency—especially if a betrayed partner becomes the deciding vote. The model’s strategic ceiling is high; the main risk is that its safest language and tidy coalition logic can read as either bland (early) or self-serving (late), and both perceptions are enough to turn a well-played board into a losing verdict.
